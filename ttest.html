Does Your Writing Hand Beat Your Other Hand?

1. What We Did

In this activity, nine students from our class wrote the sentence "I am amazing" for one minute using their writing hand and then their other hand. The class also recorded whether each student writes in script or not. Dr. Jacques completed the same activity, and her writing-hand count of 8 was used as a benchmark to compare with the class. We used the results to perform three different t-tests to see whether the differences between the averages were statistically significant.

2. My Results

Test Mean 1 Mean 2 Difference p-value
One-sample 6.33 8.00 -1.67 0.001
Independent 6.40 6.25 0.15 0.840
Paired 6.33 2.56 3.78 < 0.001

3. The Three T-Tests

One-Sample T-Test

The class wrote an average of 6.33 sentences using their writing hand, while Dr. Jacques wrote 8 sentences. This means the class wrote about 1.67 fewer sentences on average. The p-value was 0.001, which is below 0.05, so the difference was statistically significant. This suggests that the class wrote fewer sentences than Dr. Jacques's benchmark.

Independent-Samples T-Test

Students who write in script had an average of 6.40 sentences, while students who do not write in script had an average of 6.25 sentences. The difference was only 0.15 sentences. The p-value was 0.840, which is above 0.05, so the difference was not statistically significant. This means we do not have enough evidence to say that students who write in script write faster than those who do not.

Paired-Samples T-Test

The students wrote an average of 6.33 sentences using their writing hand and 2.56 sentences using their other hand. The difference was about 3.78 sentences. The p-value was less than 0.001, which is below 0.05, so the difference was statistically significant. This shows that students in our class wrote more sentences using their usual writing hand.

4. In Plain Words

Overall, the results showed that students wrote more sentences using their usual writing hand compared to their other hand. The class also wrote fewer sentences than Dr. Jacques, but there was no significant difference between students who write in script and those who do not. I think this activity shows how t-tests can help us understand whether differences between averages are statistically significant or could be due to chance.

5. What I Would Do Differently

If I could do this study again, I would include more students because nine students is a very small sample. I would also have some students start with their other hand first because practicing with the writing hand could affect their performance during the second round. Another thing I would change is to repeat the activity more than once, including for Dr. Jacques, to see whether the results are consistent. I think these changes would make the study more reliable and help us better understand the differences in writing speed.

Which Test Would You Use?

1. Blood pressure: Paired-samples t-test because the same patients had their blood pressure measured before and after the program.

2. Hours of sleep: Independent-samples t-test because the researcher is comparing two different groups of students.

3. Birth weight: One-sample t-test because one county's average is being compared to a fixed national average of 7.5 pounds.

4. Smoking and mental distress: Correlation or regression because the researcher wants to see whether two numerical variables are related across ZIP codes.

5. Resting heart rate: Paired-samples t-test because the same gym members are measured in January and June.

6. Emergency room wait times: Independent-samples t-test because the study compares averages from two different hospitals.

7. Health insurance and smoking: This needs a chi-square test of independence, which we have not learned yet. Both questions have yes or no answers, so they are categories instead of numerical averages.
